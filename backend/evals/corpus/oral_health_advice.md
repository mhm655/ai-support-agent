# Brightleaf Family Dental - Oral Health Advice and Aftercare

## Brushing

Brush twice a day for two full minutes, with a fluoride toothpaste of at
least fourteen hundred parts per million. Two minutes is much longer than
most people think, and the average unassisted brushing time is closer to
forty five seconds, so use a timer or an electric brush that has one.

Brush before breakfast rather than after, or wait an hour after eating.
Brushing immediately after anything acidic, including orange juice and
sparkling water, scrubs enamel that has been temporarily softened by the
acid.

Spit, do not rinse. Rinsing with water straight after brushing washes away
the concentrated fluoride that would otherwise keep working on the enamel.
This one change costs nothing and is the single most effective habit
adjustment we recommend.

Electric brushes with an oscillating-rotating or sonic head do remove more
plaque than a manual brush in most people's hands, and the pressure sensor
matters as much as the motion, because brushing too hard is a very common
cause of gum recession. Replace the head every three months or when the
bristles splay.

## Flossing and cleaning between teeth

Brushing cleans about sixty percent of the tooth surface. The contact points
between teeth, where most decay in adults actually starts, are only reached
by floss or an interdental brush.

Interdental brushes are more effective than floss where the gaps are big
enough to take one, and most adults with any gum disease history should be
using them rather than floss. Floss is the right choice where the contacts
are tight.

Water flossers are a reasonable option for people with braces, bridges or
implants, or anyone who will genuinely use one when they will not use floss.
They are less effective than an interdental brush at disrupting plaque on
the tooth surface but far better than nothing.

Gums that bleed when you start flossing are inflamed, and the bleeding is a
reason to keep going rather than to stop. It generally settles within one to
two weeks of daily cleaning. Bleeding that persists beyond three weeks should
be looked at.

## Diet and decay

Decay is driven by how often sugar is consumed, not by the total amount. Six
sweets eaten at one sitting does far less damage than the same six spread
across the day, because each exposure restarts an acid attack that lasts
twenty to forty minutes.

Sipping a sugary or acidic drink slowly across an afternoon is the single
worst pattern for teeth. That includes fruit juice, sports drinks, energy
drinks and sweetened coffee. Diet and sugar-free sodas contain no sugar but
are still acidic and still erode enamel.

Cheese, plain nuts and plain yoghurt are good snacks for teeth. Water is the
best drink between meals, and tap water in this area is fluoridated.

Dried fruit is sticky and stays in contact with the teeth far longer than
fresh fruit, so it is worse for teeth despite being a whole food.

## Sensitivity

Sensitivity to cold that fades within a few seconds is usually exposed
dentine, from gum recession or from brushing too hard. A desensitising
toothpaste used consistently for four to six weeks helps most cases. Rub a
little onto the sensitive area with a fingertip at night and do not rinse it
off.

Pain that lingers for more than thirty seconds after the cold is removed,
pain that wakes you at night, or pain on biting is a different problem and
should be examined. That pattern suggests the nerve is involved rather than
simple sensitivity.

## Bad breath

Most bad breath originates on the back of the tongue rather than from the
stomach. A tongue scraper used once a day is more effective than mouthwash.
Persistent bad breath despite good cleaning can indicate gum disease, a
failing restoration, or a dry mouth, and is worth mentioning at a visit.

Dry mouth is a very common side effect of many prescription medicines,
including antidepressants, antihistamines and blood pressure drugs. It
substantially raises decay risk, because saliva is the mouth's main defence.
Tell us about any medicine you are taking, since it may change how often we
want to see you.

## Mouthwash

Mouthwash is optional and is not a substitute for brushing or cleaning
between the teeth. If you use one, use it at a different time of day from
brushing, so it does not wash away the toothpaste fluoride.

Avoid alcohol-based mouthwashes for daily long-term use, particularly if you
have a dry mouth already. Chlorhexidine mouthwash is effective but stains the
teeth with prolonged use and should only be used for the short course we
prescribe it for, usually two weeks.

## Smoking and vaping

Smoking is the largest modifiable risk factor for gum disease and for oral
cancer, and it also masks the warning sign, because nicotine constricts the
blood vessels and stops the gums bleeding even when they are inflamed.

Vaping is not risk free for the mouth. Nicotine still reduces blood flow to
the gums, and propylene glycol dries the mouth. We do not yet have long-term
data comparable to what exists for cigarettes.

Smoking also substantially raises the failure rate of dental implants, which
is why we ask about it directly before planning implant treatment.

## After a filling or a cleaning

After a routine cleaning you can eat and drink immediately, unless fluoride
varnish was applied, in which case avoid hot drinks and hard food for four
hours and do not brush until the next morning.

After a filling, wait until the numbness has fully worn off before eating, to
avoid biting your lip or your tongue. Numbness usually lasts two to three
hours. If your bite feels high or uneven once the numbness goes, call us; a
filling that is even slightly proud needs a two minute adjustment and will
cause pain if it is left.
