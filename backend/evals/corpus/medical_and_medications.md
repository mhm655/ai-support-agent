# Brightleaf Family Dental - Medical History, Medications and Special Situations

## Why we ask about your medical history

We update your medical history at every visit, not to be bureaucratic, but
because a surprising number of common conditions and medicines change how
dental treatment is planned and how it heals. Please tell us about changes
even when they seem unrelated to your mouth.

## Blood thinners

Patients on warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, clopidogrel or daily aspirin
should not stop taking them before dental treatment unless the prescribing
doctor has specifically told them to. Stopping a blood thinner to have a
tooth out carries a far higher risk than the bleeding does.

For patients on warfarin we need an INR taken within seventy two hours of an
extraction, and we can usually proceed if it is below four. Local measures,
packing and suturing the socket, control the bleeding in nearly every case.

## Bisphosphonates and bone medication

Alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid and denosumab, prescribed for
osteoporosis and for some cancers, carry a small risk of osteonecrosis of
the jaw after extractions. The risk is very low for oral tablets taken for
osteoporosis and higher for intravenous forms given for cancer.

Tell us if you take or have ever taken any of these, including if you stopped
years ago, since the effect on bone is long lasting. We will usually plan to
save teeth rather than extract them where that is a realistic option.

## Diabetes

Poorly controlled diabetes and gum disease each make the other worse, and
treating the gum disease measurably improves blood sugar control. If you are
diabetic we will usually want to see you every three or four months rather
than every six.

Book morning appointments where possible, eat normally beforehand, and take
your medication as usual. Tell us your most recent HbA1c if you know it.
Healing after extractions and implant success rates are both affected by
control, which is why we ask.

## Heart conditions and antibiotic premedication

Antibiotic premedication before dental treatment is now recommended for a
much narrower group than it used to be. It applies to patients with a
prosthetic heart valve, a previous episode of infective endocarditis, certain
unrepaired congenital heart conditions, and heart transplant patients who
develop valve problems.

Routine premedication for joint replacements is no longer generally
recommended, though some orthopaedic surgeons still request it for the first
two years and we will follow the surgeon's written advice.

The standard regimen is two grams of amoxicillin taken one hour before the
appointment. Take it at home; if you forget, tell the front desk on arrival
rather than saying nothing, because it can sometimes be taken on the spot and
the appointment shifted later in the day.

## Pregnancy

Dental treatment during pregnancy is safe and routine care should not be
postponed. Cleanings and fillings are entirely fine, and gum inflammation is
extremely common in pregnancy because of hormonal changes, so cleanings
matter more rather than less.

The second trimester is the most comfortable time for longer appointments.
Dental x-rays with a lead apron and thyroid collar deliver a negligible dose
and are safe, though we defer non-urgent x-rays until after delivery as a
matter of caution rather than necessity.

Local anesthetic with lidocaine is safe in pregnancy. We avoid elective
treatment in the first trimester and avoid long appointments lying flat in
the third.

## Cancer treatment

If you are about to start chemotherapy, radiotherapy to the head or neck, or
a bone marrow transplant, we want to see you before treatment starts, ideally
at least two weeks before. Clearing infection beforehand prevents serious
complications during treatment when immunity is low.

Radiotherapy to the jaws permanently reduces the blood supply to the bone and
makes extractions afterward considerably riskier, so any tooth of doubtful
prognosis is best dealt with in advance.

Dry mouth and rapid decay are very common after head and neck radiotherapy,
and those patients are seen every three months with prescription strength
fluoride.

## Medicines that affect the mouth

Many antidepressants, antihistamines, diuretics and blood pressure medicines
cause dry mouth and raise decay risk substantially. Calcium channel blockers
such as amlodipine and nifedipine can cause the gums to overgrow. Some
inhaled asthma steroids raise the risk of oral thrush, and rinsing the mouth
after using the inhaler prevents most of it.

Bring an up-to-date list of everything you take, including over the counter
supplements, and update us when it changes.

## Allergies

Tell us about any allergy, particularly to penicillin, latex, and to local
anesthetics. A genuine allergy to local anesthetic is rare, and what people
usually recall is the racing heart from the adrenaline in the solution,
which is an expected effect rather than an allergy. We stock latex-free
gloves and materials and use them as standard.

## Anxiety and past bad experiences

A previous bad dental experience is one of the commonest reasons people stop
going, and we hear about it constantly. Tell us at booking. We can schedule
a longer appointment, agree a stop signal, avoid describing what we are
doing if you prefer not to hear it, and use nitrous oxide. There is no
judgement here about how long it has been since your last visit.
